## b) My Example

The big takeaway from this paper is that you can use data to figure out *where* accidents happen most, *why* they happen (road type, lack of education), and then make targeted fixes instead of just throwing money at the problem randomly.

I thought about how this would work if we applied it to **USC's campus**.

### The Setup

Say we collected:
- Campus police reports on parking lot fender-benders and pedestrian incidents (2019-2024)
- Shuttle bus data showing where traffic gets congested
- Records of which students took any kind of driving safety orientation

### What We'd Probably Find

**Problem spots** (like Greenville/Charleston in the paper):
- Assembly Street by the stadium - crazy traffic on game days and just in general
- Greene Street crossings - students dart across between classes all the time
- Those parking garage exits where visibility is terrible

**Infrastructure issues** (like two-way undivided roads):
- Sumter Street has no real barrier between cars and pedestrians
- The Bull Street crossings by Greek Village don't have protected signals
- Five Points is a mess of jaywalkers and cars

**Education gaps** (like Alive@25 declining in rural areas):
- Most students never take any campus driving course - it's optional and nobody bothers
- International students often don't know US traffic rules/norms
- Freshmen have the most parking lot incidents (no surprise there)

### What We'd Recommend

Same logic as the paper:
- Put up protected crossings on Greene Street
- Add barriers on Assembly Street's median  
- Make freshmen and international students do a quick driving orientation before they can register a car on campus

## c) Why This Supports the Paper's Conclusions

The paper basically makes a few key points, and my campus example hits all of them:

**1. Accidents cluster in specific places**

The paper found urban counties (Greenville, Charleston, Richland) have way more crashes than rural ones. On campus, you'd see the same thing - Assembly Street and Greene Street would dominate the incident reports while random side streets would be fine. The point is: don't spread resources thin, focus on the hot spots.

**2. Road design matters a lot**

Their data showed two-way undivided roads are the most dangerous. On campus, streets without physical barriers or protected crossings would show the same pattern. It's not rocket science - if there's nothing separating pedestrians from cars, bad things happen more often.

**3. Education programs help, but only if people actually use them**

The paper found Alive@25 participation dropping in rural areas while accidents went up. On campus, optional safety courses get ignored. Making them mandatory (or at least strongly incentivized) for high-risk groups would probably move the needle.

**4. You need different departments working together**

The paper involved SCDOT, DHEC, National Safety Council, and academic researchers all sharing data. A campus version would need USC Police, Facilities, Student Affairs, and the city of Columbia to actually talk to each other - which is easier said than done, but that's kinda the point.

**5. Recommendations should be specific, not vague**

The paper doesn't just say "make roads safer" - it says prioritize Greenville, Charleston, and Richland for divided roads with barriers. A campus analysis should do the same: "fix the Greene Street crossing first" rather than "improve pedestrian safety."

---

## Bottom Line

The paper's approach - use real data to find patterns, identify root causes, and make specific recommendations - works for state-level traffic safety and would work just as well for a university campus. It's basically the same methodology scaled down to a smaller area.
